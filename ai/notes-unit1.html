What is AI?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science that emphasizes the creation of intelligent machines that work and react like humans. It involves building smart systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and translation between languages.

Types of AI (based on operational capabilities)

TypeMeaningExample
Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI)Dedicated systems trained to perform specific tasks.Chess engines, facial recognition systems, language translation tools
Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)Theoretical AI systems capable of learning, reasoning, and applying intellectual capabilities across any human domain.Theoretical (does not exist yet)
Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)A hypothetical form of AI that surpasses human intelligence across all creative, scientific, and socio-economic domains.Hypothetical

History and Evolution Timeline

  1. 1950 (The Turing Test)Alan Turing introduced the Turing Test in his paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" to evaluate whether a machine can exhibit human-like intelligence.
  2. 1956 (Dartmouth Conference)Organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon. John McCarthy coined the term "Artificial Intelligence" and is known as the Father of AI.
  3. 1966 (ELIZA)Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA, an early natural language processing chatbot operating on pattern matching.
  4. 1974-1980 (First AI Winter)Research stalled due to severe hardware constraints, low computational memory, and reduced government funding.
  5. 1980s (Expert Systems Era)Expert systems like MYCIN (medical diagnosis) and XCON (hardware configuration) introduced rule-based decision engines using IF-THEN structures.
  6. 1987-1993 (Second AI Winter)High maintenance costs of expert systems and the failure of specialized hardware caused another period of funding cuts.
  7. 1997 (IBM Deep Blue)Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov using high-speed parallel processing and game-tree search algorithms.
  8. 2012-Present (Deep Learning and Modern Era)AlexNet won the ImageNet competition using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) on GPUs, starting the deep learning and modern Transformer/LLM era.

Foundations of Artificial Intelligence

AI is built upon concepts derived from several foundational disciplines:

  • Philosophy: Explores formal logic (Aristotle's syllogisms), rules of rational thought, mind-body dualism, and how physical brains execute logical operations.
  • Mathematics: Provides formal logic, probability theory (handling uncertainty via Bayes' Theorem), linear algebra, continuous calculus, and gradient descent optimization algorithms.
  • Cognitive Science: Studies human thought mechanisms, perception models, memory organization, and functional models of human problem-solving.

Bayes' Theorem: P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)

Basic Terms

  • Agent: An entity that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon that environment using actuators.
  • Percept: The current perceptual input collected by the agent's sensors at any given instant.
  • Percept Sequence: The complete history of all percepts perceived by an agent till date.

Types of Intelligent Agents

Agents are categorized into five structural architectures based on their decision mechanisms:

1. Simple Reflex Agent

  • Selects actions based exclusively on the current percept, completely ignoring past percept history.
  • Operates using static Condition-Action Rules (IF condition THEN action).
  • Fails in partially observable environments because it lacks memory.

2. Model-Based Reflex Agent

  • Maintains an internal state (world model) to track unobserved features of a partially observable environment.
  • Evaluates "How the world evolves" and "How my actions affect the world".

3. Goal-Based Agent

  • Combines state information with explicit Goal Information.
  • Uses search and planning algorithms (such as BFS, DFS, or A*) to find action sequences that reach target goal states.

4. Utility-Based Agent

  • Uses a quantitative Utility Function to measure how desirable a state or outcome is.
  • Evaluates trade-offs (e.g., speed, safety, cost) to select paths that maximize expected performance.

5. Learning Agent

Designed to operate in unfamiliar environments and adaptively improve performance over time through feedback.

Four Core Components:

  • Critic: Evaluates agent behavior against a fixed performance standard.
  • Learning Element: Learns from feedback to update internal decision rules.
  • Performance Element: Selects external actions.
  • Problem Generator: Proposes exploratory actions to discover improved strategies.
