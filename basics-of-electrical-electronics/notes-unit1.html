Classification of Network Elements

Classification PairDefinition & Physical MeaningExamples
1. Active vs. Passive Elements• Active Elements: Capable of delivering net electrical energy to the circuit over an infinite time interval or providing power gain/amplification.
• Passive Elements: Cannot generate energy; they either dissipate energy as heat (R) or store it temporarily in magnetic/electric fields (L, C).		Active: Voltage sources, Current sources, Generators, Batteries, Operational Amplifiers.
Passive: Resistors (R), Inductors (L), Capacitors (C), Transformers.
2. Unilateral vs. Bilateral Elements• Bilateral Elements: Voltage-current relationship remains identical for both directions of current flow (conduction is equal in both directions).
• Unilateral Elements: V-I characteristics change when the polarity of applied voltage or direction of current is reversed.		Bilateral: Resistors, Inductors, Capacitors, Transmission lines.
Unilateral: p-n Junction Diodes, Zener Diodes, BJTs, SCRs, LEDs.
3. Linear vs. Non-Linear Elements• Linear Elements: Obey the principles of superposition and homogeneity; their V-I graph is a straight line passing through the origin.
• Non-Linear Elements: Do not obey superposition; resistance/impedance changes with applied voltage or current.		Linear: Ohmic Resistors, Air-core Inductors, Ideal Capacitors.
Non-Linear: Semiconductor Diodes, Transistors, Iron-core inductors (in saturation), Thermistors.
4. Lumped vs. Distributed Elements• Lumped: Physically separable elements concentrated at single points (size ≪ wavelength).
• Distributed: Spread continuously along the entire length of the circuit.		Lumped: Breadboard R, L, C components.
Distributed: Long-distance high-voltage transmission lines.

1.3 Basic Circuit Laws: Ohm's Law, KCL & KVL (TSO 1b)

  • 1. Ohm's Law: At constant temperature and physical conditions, the current I flowing through a linear conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference V across its ends: V = I · R (or in point form: J = σ E).
    Limitations: Does not apply to unilateral/non-linear devices (diodes, transistors), electrolytes, arc lamps, or conductors whose temperature changes significantly.
  • 2. Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL — Conservation of Charge): The algebraic sum of all currents entering and leaving any node (junction) in an electrical network at any instant is zero:
    ∑ Inode = 0    ⟺    ∑ Ientering = ∑ Ileaving.
  • 3. Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL — Conservation of Energy): The algebraic sum of all branch voltages (EMFs and IR voltage drops) around any closed loop or mesh in a network at any instant is zero:
    ∑ Vloop = 0    ⟺    ∑ E = ∑ (I · R).

1.4 Series & Parallel Circuits, Voltage/Current Sources & Source Transformation (TSO 1d)

Series vs. Parallel Combinations of Resistors

  • Series Combination (Same Current I):
    Req = R1 + R2 + … + Rn
    Voltage Divider Rule (VDR): Across Rk in a series circuit:
    Vk = Vtotal × ( Rk / Req )
    For two resistors: V1 = V · R1 / (R1 + R2).
  • Parallel Combination (Same Voltage V):
    1 / Req = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + … + 1 / Rn
    For two resistors: Req = (R1 R2) / (R1 + R2)
    Current Divider Rule (CDR): For two parallel branches R1 and R2:
    I1 = Itotal × [ R2 / (R1 + R2) ] ,   I2 = Itotal × [ R1 / (R1 + R2) ].

Ideal vs. Practical Sources & Source Transformation

  • Ideal Voltage Source: Internal series resistance Rs = 0 Ω (terminal voltage remains constant regardless of load current).
  • Practical Voltage Source: Ideal voltage source Vs in series with internal resistance Rs (VL = Vs − IL Rs).
  • Ideal Current Source: Internal parallel resistance Rp = ∞ (Open).
  • Practical Current Source: Ideal current source Is in parallel with internal resistance Rp.
  • Source Transformation Equivalence: A practical voltage source (Vs in series with R) is terminal-equivalent to a practical current source (Is in parallel with the same R), where:
    Is = Vs / R    ⟺    Vs = Is · R.

Solved Example 1.1: Nodal Analysis

Problem: Find the node voltage VA and current through the middle 6 Ω resistor in a T-network where node A is connected to a 24 V source through 4 Ω, to a 12 V source through 3 Ω, and to ground through 6 Ω.

Solution: Apply KCL at principal node A (assuming all branch currents leave node A):

(VA − 24) / 4 + VA / 6 + (VA − 12) / 3 = 0

Multiply throughout by LCM = 12:

3(VA − 24) + 2VA + 4(VA − 12) = 0   ⇒   9VA − 72 − 48 = 0   ⇒   9VA = 120   ⇒   VA = 40 / 3 = 13.33 V.

Current through the 6 Ω resistor: I6Ω = VA / 6 = (40/3) / 6 = 20 / 9 = 2.22 A.

1.6 Star-Delta (Y-Δ) and Delta-Star (Δ-Y) Transformations (TSO 1d)

When a network (such as an unbalanced Wheatstone bridge) has neither series nor parallel resistor combinations, we use Delta-to-Star (Δ → Y) or Star-to-Delta (Y → Δ) equivalence between terminals A, B, and C:

  • 1. Delta to Star (Δ → Y) Conversion: Given Delta resistors RAB, RBC, RCA:
    "Star resistance connected to a node = (Product of two adjacent Delta resistors) / (Sum of all three Delta resistors)"
    RA = (RAB · RCA) / (RAB + RBC + RCA)
    RB = (RAB · RBC) / (RAB + RBC + RCA)
    RC = (RBC · RCA) / (RAB + RBC + RCA)
  • 2. Star to Delta (Y → Δ) Conversion: Given Star resistors RA, RB, RC:
    "Delta resistance between two nodes = Sum of those two Star resistors + (Their product / Opposite Star resistor)"
    RAB = RA + RB + (RA RB / RC) = (RARB + RBRC + RCRA) / RC
    RBC = RB + RC + (RB RC / RA) = (RARB + RBRC + RCRA) / RA
    RCA = RC + RA + (RC RA / RB) = (RARB + RBRC + RCRA) / RB
  • Shortcut for Equal Resistors: If all three Delta resistors are equal (RΔ), then each equivalent Star resistor is RY = RΔ / 3 (and conversely RΔ = 3 RY).
