This unit introduces the fundamental concepts required to analyse electrical circuits. It covers circuit elements, electrical laws, circuit connections, sources, network analysis and network transformation techniques.

1. Circuit Elements

An electrical circuit consists of different components through which electric current flows. These components are called circuit elements.

1.1 Resistance

Resistance is the property of an electrical material that opposes the flow of electric current. It is represented by R and its SI unit is Ohm (Ω).

V = IR

Resistance of a conductor is given by:

R = ρL / A
  • R = Resistance in ohms
  • ρ = Resistivity of material
  • L = Length of conductor
  • A = Cross-sectional area

Power Dissipated by a Resistor

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

1.2 Inductance

Inductance is the property of an electrical circuit by which it opposes any change in current flowing through it. The element possessing inductance is called an inductor. Inductance is represented by L and its SI unit is Henry (H).

v(t) = L di(t)/dt

An inductor stores electrical energy in the form of a magnetic field.

Energy stored = ½LI²

1.3 Capacitance

Capacitance is the ability of a capacitor to store electric charge. It is represented by C and its SI unit is Farad (F).

Q = CV

Current through a capacitor is:

i(t) = C dv(t)/dt

Energy stored in a capacitor is:

Energy = ½CV²

Comparison of R, L and C

Element Symbol Unit Main Property
Resistance R Ohm (Ω) Opposes current
Inductance L Henry (H) Opposes change in current
Capacitance C Farad (F) Stores electric charge

2. Classification of Circuit Elements

2.1 Active Elements

Active elements are electrical elements that can supply energy to a circuit or provide power gain/control.

Examples: Independent voltage source, independent current source and controlled sources.

2.2 Passive Elements

Passive elements cannot generate net energy. They absorb or store energy.

Examples: Resistor, inductor and capacitor.

2.3 Unilateral Elements

A unilateral element behaves differently when the direction of current or voltage is reversed.

Example: Diode.

2.4 Bilateral Elements

A bilateral element has approximately the same electrical characteristics when the direction of current is reversed.

Example: Ideal resistor.

2.5 Linear Elements

A linear element has a proportional relationship between voltage and current and obeys the principle of superposition under the applicable conditions.

Example: Constant resistance resistor.

2.6 Non-Linear Elements

A non-linear element does not have a proportional relationship between voltage and current.

Example: Semiconductor diode.

3. Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that, at constant physical conditions such as temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage applied across it.

V = IR
I = V/R     and     R = V/I
Example:
If a voltage of 20 V is applied across a 10 Ω resistor:

I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A

4. Kirchhoff's Laws

4.1 Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

KCL is based on the conservation of electric charge. It states that the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero.

ΣI = 0
Current entering a node = Current leaving the node
Example:
If 5 A enters a node and 2 A leaves through one branch, the remaining outgoing current is 5 − 2 = 3 A.

4.2 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

KVL is based on conservation of energy. It states that the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero.

ΣV = 0

The sum of voltage rises is equal to the sum of voltage drops.

5. Series and Parallel Circuits

5.1 Series Circuit

In a series circuit, components are connected one after another so that the same current flows through every element.

Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ... + Rn

The total voltage is divided among the resistors.

5.2 Parallel Circuit

In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two nodes. Therefore, the voltage across each parallel branch is the same.

1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

For two resistors in parallel:

Rtotal = (R₁R₂)/(R₁ + R₂)
Example:
For R₁ = 6 Ω and R₂ = 3 Ω in parallel:

R = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2 Ω

6. Voltage and Current Sources

6.1 Voltage Source

A voltage source maintains a specified voltage across its terminals irrespective of the current drawn by the circuit, in the ideal source model.

Examples include batteries and ideal voltage sources.

6.2 Current Source

A current source maintains a specified current through its branch, independent of the voltage across it, in the ideal source model.

Ideal and Practical Sources

Source Ideal Characteristic Practical Model
Voltage Source Internal resistance = 0 Small series resistance
Current Source Internal resistance = ∞ Large parallel resistance

7. Source Transformation

Source transformation is the process of converting a practical voltage source into an equivalent practical current source, or vice versa.

Voltage Source → Current Source

A voltage source Vs in series with resistance R can be converted into a current source Is in parallel with the same resistance.

Is = Vs/R

Current Source → Voltage Source

Vs = Is × R
Important: The resistance remains the same during source transformation, but its connection changes from series to parallel or vice versa.

8. Mesh Analysis

Mesh analysis is a systematic method used to determine currents in a planar electrical circuit. It is based on Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL).

Steps of Mesh Analysis

  1. Identify all independent meshes in the circuit.
  2. Assign a mesh current to each mesh, usually clockwise.
  3. Apply KVL to every mesh.
  4. Write voltage drops in terms of mesh currents.
  5. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  6. Determine the required branch currents or voltages.
For a resistor shared by two meshes: Voltage drop = R(I₁ − I₂)

9. Nodal Analysis

Nodal analysis is a systematic method for finding unknown node voltages in an electrical circuit. It is based on Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL).

Steps of Nodal Analysis

  1. Select a reference node (ground).
  2. Assign voltage variables to the remaining nodes.
  3. Apply KCL at each unknown node.
  4. Express branch currents using Ohm's law.
  5. Solve the resulting simultaneous equations.
  6. Calculate required currents or voltages.
Current through a resistor between nodes Va and Vb: I = (Va − Vb)/R
Remember:
Mesh Analysis → KVL → Mesh currents
Nodal Analysis → KCL → Node voltages

10. Voltage Divider Rule

The voltage divider rule is used in a series circuit to determine the voltage across an individual resistor.

For two resistors R₁ and R₂ connected in series across a voltage V:

V₂ = V × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
V₁ = V × R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
Example:
V = 12 V, R₁ = 2 Ω and R₂ = 4 Ω.

V₂ = 12 × 4/(2 + 4) = 48/6 = 8 V

11. Current Divider Rule

Current divider rule is used to determine current through individual branches of a parallel circuit.

I₁ = I × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
I₂ = I × R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
Important concept: In a parallel circuit, the branch having lower resistance carries higher current.

12. Star-Delta Transformation

Star-Delta transformation is used to simplify electrical networks that cannot be reduced directly using ordinary series and parallel combinations.

12.1 Star (Y) Network

In a star connection, three resistors have one common junction. The resistances are represented as RA, RB and RC.

12.2 Star to Delta Conversion

RAB = RA + RB + (RA × RB)/RC
RBC = RB + RC + (RB × RC)/RA
RCA = RC + RA + (RC × RA)/RB

12.3 Delta to Star Conversion

Let the delta resistances be RAB, RBC and RCA. Then:

RA = (RAB × RCA)/(RAB + RBC + RCA)
RB = (RAB × RBC)/(RAB + RBC + RCA)
RC = (RBC × RCA)/(RAB + RBC + RCA)
Exam Tip:
Star → Delta: Each delta resistance is equal to the sum of the two adjacent star resistances plus their product divided by the opposite star resistance.

Delta → Star: Each star resistance is the product of the two adjacent delta resistances divided by the sum of all three delta resistances.

15. Unit 1 Summary

Unit 1 provides the foundation for electrical circuit analysis. The student first learns the basic circuit elements such as resistance, inductance and capacitance and their classifications. Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws are then used to analyse electrical circuits.

Series and parallel combinations simplify circuits, while source transformation, mesh analysis and nodal analysis provide systematic methods for solving more complex networks. Voltage divider, current divider and Star-Delta transformations are important techniques for circuit simplification and analysis.

Mesh → KVL
