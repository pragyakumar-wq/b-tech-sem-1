Assignment-1 · Question 4 (Sub-BEEE · Submission: 21/10/2026)

Discuss the mechanism of breakdown in a diode. Differentiate between Zener and avalanche breakdown.

1. What is Reverse Breakdown in a p-n Junction Diode?

When a p-n junction diode is reverse biased, only a tiny reverse saturation current IS (in μA for Ge, nA for Si) flows due to thermally generated minority carriers. However, if the reverse bias voltage VR is increased beyond a critical threshold called the Breakdown Voltage (VBR or VZ), the reverse current increases abruptly and rapidly while the voltage across the junction remains nearly constant. This phenomenon is called Diode Breakdown.

There are two distinct physical mechanisms responsible for reverse breakdown in a diode: (1) Zener Breakdown and (2) Avalanche Breakdown.

2. Mechanism of Zener Breakdown vs. Avalanche Breakdown

1. Zener Breakdown Mechanism (VR < 6 V)

  • Heavy Doping & Narrow Depletion Layer: Occurs in heavily doped p-n junctions where the depletion region is extremely thin (width W < 10 nm).
  • Intense Electric Field: Even a small reverse voltage (2 V to 6 V) across this narrow depletion layer creates an intense electric field E = V / W ≈ 3 × 107 V/m.
  • Direct Rupture / Quantum Tunneling: This intense electric field directly pulls valence electrons out of covalent bonds in the p-side and makes them tunnel across the narrow energy barrier into the conduction band of the n-side (quantum mechanical tunneling), producing a large reverse current at constant voltage VZ.

2. Avalanche Breakdown Mechanism (VR > 6 V)

  • Light / Moderate Doping & Wider Depletion Layer: Occurs in lightly or moderately doped p-n junctions with a wider depletion zone at higher reverse voltages (typically > 6 V).
  • Carrier Acceleration & Impact Ionization: Thermally generated minority carriers crossing the depletion region are accelerated to high kinetic energy by the reverse voltage.
  • Carrier Multiplication (Avalanche Effect): These high-speed electrons collide with stationary crystal atoms, breaking covalent bonds and knocking out new electron-hole pairs (impact ionization). These secondary carriers also accelerate and create more pairs in a chain reaction (avalanche), causing a sudden surge in reverse current.
