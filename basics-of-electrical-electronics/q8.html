Assignment-1 · Question 8 (Sub-BEEE · Submission: 21/10/2026)

Explain the intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductor with suitable energy band diagrams.

1. Intrinsic Semiconductor

Definition: A semiconductor in its extremely pure form, without any impurity atoms added to its crystal lattice, is called an Intrinsic Semiconductor. Common examples are pure crystalline Silicon (Si, Z = 14) and Germanium (Ge, Z = 32).

  • Covalent Bonding Structure: Each Si or Ge atom has 4 valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds by sharing one electron with each of its four nearest neighboring atoms.
  • At Absolute Zero (T = 0 K): All valence electrons are tightly locked in covalent bonds. The Valence Band is completely full, the Conduction Band is completely empty, and the crystal acts as a perfect insulator.
  • At Room Temperature (T = 300 K): Thermal energy breaks a small fraction of covalent bonds, freeing electrons into the Conduction Band (energy gap Eg = 1.12 eV for Si and 0.72 eV for Ge) and leaving behind positively charged vacancies called holes in the Valence Band.
  • Intrinsic Carrier Concentration: Since electrons and holes are always created in pairs: n = p = ni, and the Fermi level EF = (EC + EV)/2 lies exactly in the middle of the forbidden energy gap.

2. Extrinsic Semiconductor (Doping & Classification)

Because the electrical conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor is very low at room temperature, a small controlled amount of suitable impurity (1 atom per 106 to 108 host atoms) is added to it. This process is called Doping, and the resulting doped semiconductor is called an Extrinsic Semiconductor.

Depending on the type of doping impurity (Dopant) added, extrinsic semiconductors are classified into two types:

(a) N-Type Extrinsic Semiconductor

  • Dopant Added: Pentavalent (Group V) impurity atoms having 5 valence electrons, such as Phosphorus (P), Arsenic (As), or Antimony (Sb).
  • Mechanism (Donor Impurity): Four of the five valence electrons form covalent bonds with four adjacent Si atoms. The fifth electron is very loosely bound (requires only 0.05 eV in Si or 0.01 eV in Ge) and becomes a free conduction electron, leaving behind an immobile positively charged donor ion (+). Since each impurity atom donates one free electron, it is called a Donor Impurity.
  • Carriers & Energy Band: Electrons are Majority Carriers (n ≈ ND ≫ p) and holes are Minority Carriers. A discrete Donor Energy Level (ED) appears just below the Conduction Band EC, and the Fermi level EF shifts upward close to EC.

(b) P-Type Extrinsic Semiconductor

  • Dopant Added: Trivalent (Group III) impurity atoms having 3 valence electrons, such as Boron (B), Gallium (Ga), Aluminum (Al), or Indium (In).
  • Mechanism (Acceptor Impurity): The three valence electrons form covalent bonds with three neighboring Si atoms, while the fourth bond has a deficiency of one electron — creating a Hole. An electron from a neighboring covalent bond can easily jump into this vacancy by absorbing a tiny energy (~0.05 eV), creating an immobile negatively charged acceptor ion (−). Hence it is called an Acceptor Impurity.
  • Carriers & Energy Band: Holes are Majority Carriers (p ≈ NA ≫ n) and electrons are Minority Carriers. A discrete Acceptor Energy Level (EA) appears just above the Valence Band EV, and the Fermi level EF shifts downward close to EV.

3. Energy Band & Crystal Lattice Diagrams

Mass-Action Law & Electrical Neutrality: In both intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors at thermal equilibrium, the product of electron and hole concentrations is constant: n · p = ni2. Furthermore, the crystal as a whole remains electrically neutral because the charge of mobile carriers is balanced by immobile donor/acceptor ions: n + NA− = p + ND+.
